00

What changed in this revision

The first draft made two structural proposals. One was rejected and one was overtaken by information. Both changes improve the design, and both are load-bearing enough that the rest of the document is built on them rather than patched around them.

Change 1 — the road is chained, not anchored

The first draft proposed universal anchors: every section entering and exiting at the same fixed coordinates so any piece joins any other. That was rejected, correctly — "the key is in the word winding". The road instead stays genuinely chained, and the join is guaranteed by a server-issued template: when you reserve a slot, you are handed the exact entry and exit points your artwork must honour. §4 rebuilds the layout around this.

Change 2 — sections are canvases, not line drawings

The first draft treated the road as a stroked line that recolours with the theme. It isn't. Contributors get a rectangle and may fill the whole of it, so a section is an opaque raster image the site does not restyle. Dark mode applies to the page around the artwork, never to the artwork. That kills the currentColor SVG recommendation entirely and changes the palette reasoning, the image pipeline, and the accessibility argument. §3.1, §8 and §7 are rewritten accordingly.

Two smaller corrections: the loading mechanics are not worked out elsewhere yet — the page structure comes first and defines what the loader has to work with, so §8 now specifies that contract rather than deferring to it. And the gallery metaphor governs the space, not the vocabulary: no plaques, no "accession", nothing skeuomorphic.

Added in revision 3

The full answers revealed a piece of the system the earlier drafts missed entirely: the templates are not simply copied from the section below. A routing algorithm chooses them, steering the road toward custom-template sections waiting in a queue. That is the most technically interesting part of the project and §4.3 now covers it.

Also new: permalinks use UUIDs (§8.2, with one correction to the conclusion drawn from that), an explicit security section (§11) since it's a stated priority, and a decisions for Damian section (§12) collecting the four questions that need his eye, written so they can be read standalone.

01

Where the prototype stands

Two hardcoded rows, a 100-column grid, and a resize-driven text clamp. It renders — but almost every decision in it is a placeholder. Ordered by how much each constrains what comes next.

1.1Mobile users get no content at allBlocks design

Below the md breakpoint, both the index column and the entire text panel carry hidden. A phone visitor sees a column of drawings with no number, no title, no author, no description — the whole point of the site, absent. Content parity across viewports is not optional, and this is now the most serious defect in the file.

1.2Section widths are inconsistent, so nothing can line upBlocks design

Row one splits 25/50/25; row two splits 20/60/20. The first road renders at 50% of the viewport and the second at 60%, and both are stretched with w-full h-full, discarding their aspect ratios by different amounts. With a chained road this is fatal rather than untidy: if the artwork column is not one exact width for every section, no template can guarantee a join. §4.1 rebuilds the grid around a single width token.

1.3The existing artwork proves why templates are neededInforms design

Measured from the committed PNGs by sampling the first and last raster rows: Road_1 exits at x=213/390 and enters at x=290/466; Road_2 exits at x=312/450 and enters at x=214/389. So Road_1 → Road_2 matches to within a pixel — they were drawn as a pair — while Road_2 → Road_1 steps sideways by 22px and 16px.

That is exactly the behaviour the template system exists to prevent: hand-chaining works only when both pieces are authored together, which is never true of strangers. It also shows the road width drifting — Road_2 narrows to 138 units at its exit against 175–177 elsewhere — which is fine under a template that carries both edges independently, and impossible under one that assumes a constant width. Both files are throwaway test art and need no conversion.

1.4There is no page — only a listShapes design

No masthead, no logo slot, no <h1>, no footer, no add-a-section entry point, no theme control. The document has exactly one heading level — two <h2>s floating in a bare <main> — so anyone arriving with a screen reader gets no orientation at all.

1.5Dark mode is accidentalShapes design

DaisyUI ships a prefers-color-scheme: dark block that sets color-scheme: dark on the root, so the browser flips its default background. The template sets bg-transparent and uses no theme token anywhere, so nothing else responds. There is no toggle, no persistence, and no system/light/dark tri-state.

1.6The clamp script will not survive the loaderShapes design

A ResizeObserver watches <body> and, on every fire, loops every image reading offsetHeight and getComputedStyle, then writes inline styles — a read-write-read layout thrash, O(n) in loaded sections, on an element whose height the loop itself changes. Invisible at two rows; a stutter on every scroll-triggered load at four hundred. §5.4 replaces it with no JavaScript at all.

1.7Images declare no intrinsic sizeShapes design

No width/height attributes and no aspect-ratio, so each image reflows the page as it decodes. Under a scroll-driven loader that is not cosmetic — it moves the reader's position under their thumb as sections resolve.

1.8No design system existsShapes design

No typefaces (Tailwind's default font-sans stack), no palette, no spacing scale, no motion language. Sizes are set per-element with five breakpoint variants each — md:text-2xl lg:text-3xl xl:text-4xl 2xl:text-5xl — a type scale spelled out longhand at every call site instead of defined once.

1.9Structural class names carry no meaningHousekeeping

first-child through fourth-child describe position in a tree that will change, shadow the real CSS pseudo-classes they're named after, and are load-bearing for the clamp script's querySelector calls. Separately col-span-0 compiles to grid-column: span 0, which is invalid — browsers clamp it to 1. It does nothing; the neighbouring hidden is doing all the work.

1.10Loose endsHousekeeping

Eight of the ten files in static/images/ are orphans from earlier experiments, totalling ~390KB. README.md is UTF-16 and contains one line. SECRET_KEY is hardcoded and ALLOWED_HOSTS is empty. tests.py is the stock stub. The page <script> is inline in the content block rather than a static module in extra_js. Templates and static files are not namespaced under render/, so any other app can shadow index.html.

02

The direction: a modern gallery for art

Not a gallery of modern art — a modern gallery, hung with drawings. The metaphor governs the space and the restraint. It does not govern the vocabulary, and nothing on the page should look like a physical object.

A gallery does three things worth copying. It gives each work far more empty wall than the work occupies. It keeps its own signage rigorously consistent and rigorously quiet. And it numbers everything, because an inventory number is how a collection admits it is a collection.

What it does not do here is dress up. No engraved plaque beside each piece, no serif museum labels, no "accession no.", no faux-material textures. The register is modern web design executed with gallery discipline — which is a harder and better target than either on its own.

The constraint that decides most of this

Every section is a full-colour image chosen by a stranger, and the site never restyles it. Four hundred uncontrolled artworks stacked in a column is already an enormous amount of visual noise. So the chrome must recede — not as taste, but as function. A loud accent, a decorated frame, or a heavy type treatment would be competing with the content for attention and would lose. Everything in §3 follows from this.

The personality lives in three places, none of which fight the artwork: the typography, the scale contrast between a large inventory numeral and small quiet metadata, and the premise itself — one continuous road drawn by hundreds of strangers, presented with complete seriousness. That last one is genuinely funny because the presentation doesn't wink at it.

Do
  • Hairlines and alignment instead of borders and cards
  • Neutral chrome that lets the artwork be the only colour
  • Large inventory numerals; small, plain metadata
  • Generous asymmetric whitespace — the wall, not padding
  • Off-white and warm charcoal grounds, never #fff or #000
  • Motion that is short, interruptible, and only on interaction
Don't
  • Museum vocabulary in the UI — "plaque", "hang", "accession"
  • Anything skeuomorphic: engraved labels, paper texture, frames
  • Cards with shadow-lg and rounded-xl on a grey ground
  • Gradient headlines, glassmorphism, backdrop-blur, glow borders
  • Emoji as iconography; Inter or Space Grotesk as the face
  • A centred hero with two pill buttons under it
  • Scroll-triggered entrance animations on every section

Design system

3.1 · Palette

Still open, and this proposal is deliberately quieter than the first draft's. Paper ground was confirmed as the right instinct. The neutrals are cool and very slightly green — plaster under daylight-balanced gallery lighting rather than warm paper stock, which matters because warm cream will cast against whatever colours contributors use.

The accent is road-sign green: thematically apt twice over, legible on both grounds, and not the vermilion-on-cream that generated design defaults to. Critically it appears on interface only — links, focus rings, the add-a-section tile — and never as a decorative wash, because it has to coexist with 400 artworks it cannot predict.

The artwork does not theme

Sections are the contributor's canvas and the site must not filter, invert, dim, or recolour them in dark mode. Doing so would be editing someone's drawing. This means the artwork column stays bright against a dark wall at night — which is exactly what a gallery looks like after hours, and is fine. What it does require is that the seam between artwork and page be handled deliberately: see §5.3.

Set in outline — transparent fill, hairline stroke — so it sits back on the wall like stencilled signage instead of shouting over the drawing beside it. It fills solid in --accent when its section is hovered, focused, or expanded. One rule, and it is the most characterful thing on the page.

Numbers keep growing past 9999 rather than switching format. Pad to four digits as a minimum and let five-digit numbers simply be wider — tabular figures mean the column stays aligned either way.

3.3 · Spacing and motion

One 4px-based scale, with two rules that matter more than the numbers. The road stack has zero gap — sections butt together with no margin, border, or rounding, or the chain visibly breaks. Everything outside the road is generous to the point of feeling slightly empty. That contrast between a tight unbroken column of artwork and a lot of quiet wall is what makes it read as a hang rather than a feed.

Issue #1 asks for transitions that are "quick and elegantly cancellable". That phrasing points at a specific implementation: CSS transitions, not keyframe animations and not JS tweens. An interrupted transition retargets from its current computed value automatically, so cancellability is correct by construction. A keyframe animation restarts; a JS tween has to be unwound by hand.

  • 150–220ms for state changes, 260ms for the mobile disclosure. Nothing longer.
  • ease-out entering, ease-in leaving.
  • Transition transform, opacity and grid-template-rows only. Never height, top or width.
  • No scroll-triggered reveals. Sections appear because they loaded, not because they performed.
  • Full prefers-reduced-motion: reduce path.
04

Layout architecture

4.1 · The spine

Replace the 100-column percentage grid with a three-track grid built around one artwork-width token. The artwork is a fixed track; the flanks absorb everything else. This is what makes "fixed width, variable height" true rather than aspirational — and under a chained road it is a correctness requirement, not a preference.

.road {
  --road-w: clamp(17.5rem, 42vw, 34rem);
  display: grid;
  grid-template-columns:
    [num-start] minmax(0, 1fr) [num-end art-start]
    var(--road-w) [art-end meta-start]
    minmax(0, 1fr) [meta-end];
  row-gap: 0;              /* non-negotiable */
}
.road-meta { max-inline-size: 36ch; }

Both flanks are 1fr so the artwork stays optically centred; the metadata is capped at 36ch so it never sprawls into an unreadable measure on a wide monitor. The numeral is right-aligned against the artwork's left edge and the metadata left-aligned against its right edge — the road is the axis everything hangs off.

4.2 · The chain contract

Universal anchors are out. The road genuinely winds, and the join is guaranteed by data rather than by a constant.

Every section carries four numbers describing where the road meets its top and bottom edges: entry_l, entry_r, exit_l, exit_r, expressed as fractions of the artwork width so they are resolution-independent. The chain rule is simply:

section[n].exit_l == section[n+1].entry_l
section[n].exit_r == section[n+1].entry_r

Nothing about that is enforced by the artwork itself — it is enforced by the template the server issues at reservation time. Reserve a slot and you are told the exact coordinates your drawing must start and finish at. Because the width may drift between entry and exit (§1.3 shows the existing art already does this), the two edges are carried independently rather than as a centreline plus a width.

Vacant slots are normal operation

Slots are allocated lowest-available-first, a removed section frees its slot for reassignment, and a reservation holds a slot before the artwork exists. The chain therefore contains slots with no artwork in them, mid-road, as a matter of routine — and the top of the road is the highest-numbered completed section, not the highest-numbered reserved one.

Placeholders are generated automatically: a vacant slot cannot be skipped, because its neighbours' anchors were issued against it. It draws the connecting road its template requires, in chrome rather than artwork. That makes it the best acquisition surface on the site — every gap is an invitation to fill it, and it links straight into the reservation flow. §5.5 specifies the two states.

4.3 · Routing: how a template gets chosen

This is the part that makes the project genuinely interesting, and it is the part that is least designed. It is also, correctly, being left until the main submission flow works.

Templates are not simply inherited from the section below. Contributors who already have artwork — a photo that happens to match a certain format, or a section drawn before signing up — can submit a custom template specifying the entry and exit points and width they need. Those go into a queue, and the road is then steered toward them: the routing algorithm treats queued custom templates as waypoints, winds gradually toward each one, and arrives at exactly the geometry that section requires.

So the algorithm's job, each time a standard slot is allocated, is to choose an exit that (a) continues smoothly from the current entry, (b) makes progress toward the next waypoint, and (c) stays inside the canvas. Everything below is design consideration rather than specification — the algorithm itself is deliberately deferred.

Constraints worth fixing before writing it

  • Movement budget. The road can only shift so far laterally within one section before it stops looking like a road. Express this as a maximum lateral change per unit of height, and the whole problem becomes tractable — reachability is then computable rather than guessed.
  • Width converges too. Waypoints specify width as well as position, so the steering is two-dimensional: centreline and width. Width should change more slowly than position; a road that rapidly narrows reads as a mistake rather than a bend.
  • Containment. The road must stay within the canvas with a margin. This is a hard constraint on steering and the most likely source of visible ugliness if it's added late.
  • It isn't quite the travelling salesman. Classic TSP assumes free travel between nodes; here travel is constrained by the movement budget and the visiting order is fixed by slot number. It's closer to trajectory planning with via-points — and a greedy "nearest reachable waypoint first" will almost certainly be good enough. Worth knowing so you don't reach for a heavier algorithm than the problem needs.
  • Starvation. A waypoint whose required width sits far from the road's current width may never be selected. Age queue entries and raise their priority over time, or some contributor waits forever.
  • Feasibility at submission. Check a custom template is reachable at all when it's submitted — width within global bounds, position inside the canvas — and reject it immediately with a clear reason. Queuing something that can never be placed is the worst outcome for the contributor.
  • Issued templates are frozen. Once a template has been handed to a reserved slot it cannot change, or someone's half-finished drawing stops fitting. The algorithm plans forward only and never revises history.
  • Deliberate imperfection. Pure shortest-path steering produces a road that looks mechanical. Add bounded randomness to the curve between waypoints — the site is called The Long And Winding Road, and a road that only bends when it has a reason to is the wrong road.
Worth saying plainly

This algorithm is the strongest portfolio artefact in the project. Everything else here is careful, conventional web engineering done well; this is a constrained path-planning problem with a real aesthetic objective, arising naturally from the product rather than bolted on. When it's built, it deserves a written explanation with diagrams — that write-up will be worth more than the rest of the front page put together.

4.4 · Section height

Store the artwork's pixel dimensions on the model and reserve space from them before the image loads:

<li class="road-section" style="--ar: {{ section.width }} / {{ section.height }}">
  <figure class="road-art" style="aspect-ratio: var(--ar)">

Zero layout shift, and — more importantly — the loader can compute the height of a section it has not fetched yet, which is what makes placeholder-based unloading possible later. The allowed height range is a decision for Damian; §12.2 sets out what actually constrains it, which turns out not to be aesthetics.

05

Components

5.1 · Masthead

The logo is coming later, so build the slot now and fill it with type. Wrap it in the page's only <h1>; when the SVG arrives, inline it (not <img>) with role="img" and a <title> reading "The Long And Winding Road", so it can take currentColor and invert with the theme.

A roughly 4:3 stacked wordmark is a tall object for a masthead. Reserve the box with aspect-ratio from day one so the swap doesn't reflow the page, and cap its height in viewport units on small screens — otherwise the road starts below the fold on a phone, which is the one thing the front page cannot afford.

The theme control sits top-right, quiet, as a three-state group (System / Light / Dark) — a fieldset of radios styled as a segmented control, keyboard-operable and correctly announced without ARIA gymnastics. Default is System.

5.2 · The head tile

"Add a section" is not a button floating above the road — it is the first tile of the chain, and the road begins inside it. A short stub descends and terminates at whatever the current top section's entry_* anchors are, so it joins the road exactly like any other pair. The whole tile is one <a>.

This is where the page's single flourish goes: on hover and focus the stub extends downward a few units and the tile's ground shifts to --accent at low opacity. 180ms, transform only, reduced-motion safe. Nothing else on the page animates on hover.

5.3 · Road section

Semantically an ordered list — the road is a sequence and the numbers are load-bearing. Slot 1 sits at the bottom, so the list counts down the page:

<ol class="road" reversed>
  <li class="road-section" value="1631">
    <span class="road-index" aria-hidden="true">#1631</span>
    <figure class="road-art">
      <img src="…" alt="" width="1600" height="1200" decoding="async" loading="lazy">
      <figcaption class="road-meta">…</figcaption>
    </figure>
  </li>
</ol>

The numeral is aria-hidden because <li value> already conveys position; without that, every section announces its number twice.

Where artwork meets wall

Because the artwork is opaque and unthemed, the vertical edges of the column are a hard boundary between someone's drawing and the page. Left raw it reads as an accident, particularly in dark mode. The fix is a hairline rather than a frame: a 1px --rule line down each side of the artwork column, continuous for the whole road rather than per-section. It reads as a hanging rail, costs nothing, and keeps every section visually seated without touching the images themselves.

5.4 · Section metadata

Title, author, description, actions — set as plain modern UI, not as a label object. Aligned to the top of its section rather than vertically centred, with position: sticky inside the section so that on a tall drawing it stays with you as you scroll past.

Replacing the clamp script

Delete the ResizeObserver. The description clamps to a fixed line count in pure CSS, and overflow becomes disclosure rather than truncation:

.road-desc {
  display: -webkit-box;
  -webkit-box-orient: vertical;
  -webkit-line-clamp: 6;
  overflow: hidden;
}

Clamping to the image height was the wrong target anyway — it made the amount of a stranger's writing you could read depend on how tall they happened to draw. A fixed six lines plus "Read more" is more consistent, more legible, and costs zero JavaScript and zero layout reads per scroll. Field maximums should be set from this layout and then enforced on the backend: roughly 70 characters for a title, 40 for an author name, and 600 for a description are the numbers this design supports comfortably.

5.5 · Vacant and reserved slots

Two states, both rendered as chrome rather than artwork, both satisfying the chain contract by drawing the connecting road themselves.

StateTreatmentBehaviour
Vacant--paper-2 ground, connecting road stroked in --ink-3, label "This stretch is unbuilt"The whole tile is a link into the reservation flow. Real target, real focus state.
ReservedSame, road stroked in --ochre, label "Reserved — being drawn"Not a link. Announces that someone is working on it, which is a stronger social signal than an empty gap.

Both must carry their state as text, not colour alone. Both are ordinary <li> items in the list with their slot number, so numbering stays continuous and the loader treats them like any other section.

5.6 · The end of the road

The chain terminates at slot 1 with a title card — "The End", in the manner of an old film. It is the one place the design is allowed to be openly fond of itself. It is also the only fixed landmark on an otherwise unbounded page, which makes it worth linking to: a persistent "Jump to the end of the road" control in the masthead.

That control is not a nicety. An infinite list makes everything below it permanently unreachable by keyboard, which is a genuine WCAG failure rather than a nitpick. The corollary is that nothing essential may live below the road — no footer navigation, no legal links. Those belong in the masthead or on their own pages.

5.7 · Actions

Small uppercase mono text controls in --ink-2 at the foot of the metadata block. Always visible — never hover-revealed, which fails keyboard users, touch users and the 2.2 target-size rule simultaneously.

Confirmed: Report, which opens a dialog asking for a category and free text. Both permalinks belong here too — one to the section's own page and one that jumps to it in place — and they should be a single Share control that exposes both rather than two competing links.

6.1 · The mobile disclosure

Issue #1 describes the sections above and below tweening apart to make room. There is a subtlety worth designing around: if space opens above the tapped section, the thing the reader just touched slides out from under their thumb. So open downward only. The tapped section's top edge never moves; the drawer grows below it and pushes everything underneath down. Same visual effect, no disorientation, and no scroll-restoration code to get wrong.

.road-drawer { display: grid; grid-template-rows: 0fr;
  transition: grid-template-rows 260ms ease-out; }
.road-section[data-expanded] .road-drawer { grid-template-rows: 1fr; }
.road-drawer > div { overflow: hidden; }

@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  .road-drawer { transition-duration: 1ms; }
}

The 0fr → 1fr transition animates to auto height, is cheap, and retargets cleanly when interrupted — the "elegantly cancellable" requirement satisfied by construction rather than by bookkeeping.

  • The trigger is a real <button> wrapping the artwork, carrying aria-expanded and aria-controls.
  • Accordion behaviour — one open at a time — with the previously open section collapsing in the same frame.
  • Drawer content is always in the DOM, only visually collapsed. Never hidden, never display: none, so it stays findable by in-page search and correctly announced.
  • Don't move focus on expand; the drawer follows the button in DOM order, so the next Tab lands in it naturally.
  • <details name="road"> gives exclusive-accordion behaviour with no JS and is worth a prototype, but its animation story is still uneven across browsers — the button plus grid-rows route is the safe default.
07

Accessibility

Target is WCAG 2.2 AA, self-imposed and verified rather than assumed. Two things make this harder than average: it is an unbounded list, and its content is an image supplied by a stranger that the site has agreed not to alter.

Structure

  • Skip link to the road as the first focusable element.
  • Exactly one <h1> (the masthead). Section titles are <h2>. No level skipped.
  • The road is <ol reversed>; each section an <li value="N"> containing a <figure> and <figcaption>.
  • One <header>, one <main>, one <footer>. The prototype's duplicate <main> must not return.

Alt text, explained plainly

The first draft asked this badly. Here is the actual choice. Every <img> needs an alt attribute. It can either describe the picture, or it can be empty — alt="" — which tells a screen reader "skip this image, it isn't the content". Empty is not the same as missing: a missing alt makes screen readers read out the filename, which is the worst outcome.

Recommendation: alt="" on the artwork, with the caption carrying the meaning. The author has already written a title and a description of their own drawing, and that text sits in the <figcaption> immediately after the image. A screen reader user reaches it as ordinary text. Duplicating it into alt would make them hear it twice.

If a moderator later adds a description, put it in the alt then. Do not auto-generate alt by concatenating the title and description — that is exactly the double-announcement problem. And never ship a placeholder like alt="image1", which is worse than empty.

Contrast, given the site does not control the artwork

WCAG contrast requirements apply to text and interface, not to photographs or artwork. Since the site never overlays text on a section image, the artwork is out of scope — which is the technical reason the "don't restyle the canvas" decision costs nothing in compliance terms. Everything the site does control still has to pass: 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text and UI boundaries, checked in both themes.

Unbounded list

  • aria-busy="true" on the list while a batch is in flight.
  • A polite live region announcing loaded ranges after each batch — not on every scroll event.
  • A "jump to the end of the road" control, because the bottom of the list is otherwise unreachable.
  • A no-JS path (§8), which also makes the road crawlable.
  • Never set overflow-anchor: none on the road — browser scroll anchoring is doing useful work.

Visual and interaction

  • :focus-visible ring at 2px --accent with 2px offset, contrast-checked against both grounds.
  • Targets ≥ 44×44 CSS px (2.2 AA requires 24; take 44).
  • Reflow to 320px and zoom to 400% with no horizontal scrolling.
  • No information by colour alone — vacant and reserved slots carry text labels.
  • Full prefers-reduced-motion path.

Verification

axe-core in CI against a page rendered with ~50 sections; Lighthouse accessibility ≥ 95; and one manual screen-reader pass per platform, exercising expand, collapse and a scroll-triggered load. Automated tools catch roughly a third of real failures — the manual pass is the one that matters. Free readers: Orca (preinstalled on most Linux desktops, so available immediately), NVDA (Windows, free and open source), VoiceOver (built into macOS and iOS), TalkBack (built into Android). Between a Linux machine and a phone you can cover three of the four at no cost.

08

Images, performance, and what the loader needs

The loader is not designed yet, and the page structure is meant to come first and define what it works with. This section is that contract. The artwork column is also now essentially the entire page weight, so the image pipeline matters more than everything else combined.

8.1 · Image format

The plan currently says the uploads will "probably be JPEGs". JPEG is the wrong default for this content. It has no transparency and its block-based compression produces visible ringing around the hard edges and flat colour that hand-drawn sections are made of — exactly the artefacts you'd notice on a black road line against a flat field.

Since every upload has to be processed server-side anyway — dimensions validated, anchors checked, aspect ratio constrained — transcoding is nearly free to add at the same point:

  • Accept PNG, JPEG and WebP on upload. Keep the contributor's original untouched.
  • Serve AVIF with a WebP fallback via <picture>. Both are supported across all current major browsers, and AVIF is dramatically better than JPEG on flat-colour illustration.
  • Generate two or three widths and use srcset/sizes, since --road-w varies from about 280px to 544px.
  • Strip EXIF on re-encode. Phone photos carry GPS coordinates, and a project that wants minimal data should not be republishing a contributor's location.
  • Guard the decoder. Set Image.MAX_IMAGE_PIXELS, cap upload size, and validate dimensions before processing — a decompression bomb is the standard attack against any service that accepts images.

Pillow is sufficient at this scale and is already a Django dependency for ImageField. If throughput ever becomes a problem, pyvips is several times faster at a fraction of the memory, but starting there would be premature.

On people disliking WebP

The complaint is real but it is about saving, not viewing: someone right-clicks an image, gets a .webp, and finds their older software won't open it. Nobody objects to WebP they never have to handle.

That maps cleanly onto a fix. Serve AVIF/WebP for display, where the bandwidth saving is the whole point, and give each section an explicit Download action that hands over the contributor's original file in the format they uploaded. That's better than the status quo in both directions — visitors get fast pages, and anyone who actually wants the artwork gets a real PNG or JPEG rather than whatever the browser happened to cache. It also fits the permissive-licence intent: if the work is meant to be reusable, make it properly downloadable rather than something to be extracted from a page.

8.2 · What the loader requires from the markup

  • One markup source. A single _section.html partial renders both the server-side first page and every subsequently loaded batch. Return rendered HTML fragments from the server rather than JSON — it keeps markup in one place and removes client-side templating entirely.
  • Height before content. Dimensions on the model, aspect-ratio on the container, so a section can be sized before it is fetched.
  • A cursor, not a page number. Paginate on slot number (?before=1631), never on offset — offsets shift when a slot is filled or removed mid-scroll and silently duplicate or skip sections. Note this leaks nothing: the slot number is printed on every section as #1631, so it is already public. The UUID protects the permalink, not the ordering.
  • Offscreen sections cost nothing. content-visibility: auto with contain-intrinsic-size on every section. Measure whether this removes the need for explicit unloading before building unloading.
  • A no-JS path is still available, despite the UUID decision — see the note below.
  • If the URL updates on scroll, use history.replaceState, throttled — pushState would stack an entry per section and destroy the back button.

8.3 · Budget

First load under 100KB of CSS and JS compressed, CLS of 0 at all times, and 60fps scrolling with 200 sections in the DOM. Removing DaisyUI and the clamp script gets most of the way there before anything is optimised. Artwork is excluded from that budget and governed instead by lazy loading and format: only the first two or three sections should be fetched eagerly, with fetchpriority="high" on the first and loading="lazy" on everything below the fold.

UUID permalinks don't actually force JS-only pagination

UUID URLs are a sound choice — they stop anyone walking /1, /2, /3 to enumerate every section, and they cost nothing. But the conclusion drawn from them, that JS pagination therefore becomes required, doesn't follow, and it's worth separating the two.

A no-JS "next" link is just an anchor whose href carries a cursor: ?before=1631, or ?before=<uuid> if you'd rather. Following it returns the next batch. That grants a scraper nothing extra, because they can only get batch n+1 by first having batch n — exactly the same walk a scroller performs. Enumeration was the thing UUIDs prevented, and a cursor link doesn't reintroduce it.

Meanwhile requiring JS buys close to nothing defensively: a headless browser is a few lines of Python, and anyone scraping seriously already uses one. So the cost lands entirely on people using screen readers, search crawlers, and anyone on a flaky connection — while the determined scraper is unaffected.

What actually reduces load is the boring set: cache at the edge, rate-limit per IP, and publish the bulk export you already intend to provide, so anyone wanting the dataset takes the cheap path rather than crawling. A documented dump plus a permissive licence removes the incentive far more effectively than any technical barrier.

Going JS-only anyway is a legitimate call — it is less to build. If you do, at minimum keep the individual section pages server-rendered and crawlable, so sections remain findable and shareable even when the road itself isn't walkable without JavaScript.

09

Technical decisions

9.1 · Tokens and theming

Define the palette, type and scale once in Tailwind v4's @theme block. That publishes them as both CSS custom properties and Tailwind utilities, so bg-paper and var(--color-paper) stay in sync from one source.

@theme {
  --color-paper: #F6F8F6;
  --color-ink:   #14181A;
  --color-accent:#1E5B3E;
  --font-display: "Fraunces", var(--font-serif);
  --font-mono: "IBM Plex Mono", ui-monospace, monospace;
}
@custom-variant dark (&:where([data-theme=dark], [data-theme=dark] *));

Theme is set via data-theme on <html> with a small synchronous inline script in <head> reading localStorage then matchMedia. It must be inline and blocking, or the page flashes the wrong theme on every load.

9.2 · Sass

Sass appeared in the brief because it is the CSS tooling one of you already knows, not because anything needs it — that is a completely reasonable reason to have written it down, and it deserves a real answer rather than a preference.

Recommendation: don't add Sass. Not because Sass is bad, but because the three things people reach for it for are now native:

Sass featureModern equivalent
Variables ($brand)CSS custom properties — and unlike Sass variables these are live at runtime, so they can respond to data-theme. Sass variables are compiled away and cannot.
NestingNative CSS nesting, supported in every current browser. postcss-nested is already installed as a belt-and-braces fallback.
Partials and @use@import in the PostCSS chain, which the project already does.

The worry behind the question — "we may need custom CSS where Tailwind doesn't reach" — is correct, and the answer is simply a components/ directory of plain .css files imported into the bundle. Everything in this document that isn't a utility (the spine grid, the numeral outline, the drawer transition) lives there. It is ordinary CSS, nested, using the same tokens, and it requires no extra build step.

Worth knowing if Tailwind is unfamiliar: v4 is configured in CSS, not in a JavaScript config file. The @theme block above is the entire configuration surface. Someone who knows CSS well is much closer to knowing Tailwind v4 than they would have been with v3.

9.3 · DaisyUI

Flagged as the other contributor's call, so this is the case rather than a decision. DaisyUI is currently installed, emitting about twenty colour tokens into the bundle, and used by zero classes in the template.

The tension is that DaisyUI's value is pre-built components with a consistent look, and a consistent look is precisely what §2 rules out — its default styling is the generic register the project is explicitly avoiding. It also ships a parallel token system (--color-base-*, --color-primary) that will sit awkwardly beside the @theme tokens above, and two sources of truth for colour is a reliable source of drift.

The honest middle path, if it stays: use DaisyUI only for interactive primitives that are genuinely fiddly to build accessibly — the report dialog, the theme switcher — and never for the road, the metadata or the masthead. If it isn't being used for that, removing it is free.

9.4 · JavaScript approach

The interactivity on this page amounts to two things: a disclosure accordion and a scroll-triggered loader. That is a small surface, and it should stay small.

OptionCostWhat it givesVerdict
Vanilla ES modules~150 lines of DOM code; no build step, no dependencyFull control, nothing to learn that isn't transferable, best fit for a hand-rolled loaderRecommended
htmx (~14KB)One script tagServer returns HTML fragments; hx-trigger="revealed" is close to infinite scroll for free. Very Django-idiomatic.Worth a prototype — it does almost exactly this job
Alpine.js (~15KB)One script tagDeclarative state in markup; tidies the accordion if the vanilla version gets messyReasonable later, unnecessary now
React / VueBuild toolchain, hydration, templates duplicated between Django and JS, client routingNothing this project needsNot recommended

To answer the question behind the question: React and Vue exist to keep a complex client-side UI in sync with client-side state. This site's state lives in a database and is rendered by Django, so a framework would mean maintaining the same section markup twice and fighting for the server-rendered, no-JS, crawlable behaviour that comes free otherwise. They are excellent tools aimed at a different shape of problem. Vanilla DOM work here is also the more useful thing to have learned — framework knowledge dates, IntersectionObserver doesn't.

One specific pointer for the loader: use IntersectionObserver on a sentinel element near the foot of the list rather than listening to scroll. It fires off the main thread, needs no throttling, and is the standard approach.

What htmx actually is, since it has a reputation

htmx is one <script> tag of about 14KB. There is no npm, no build step, no compiler and no new language — you add attributes to HTML you already have. That's the whole of it. The infinite scroll, complete, is:

<div hx-get="/road/?before=1631"
     hx-trigger="revealed"
     hx-swap="outerHTML"></div>

"When this element is scrolled into view, GET that URL and replace me with whatever comes back." The server returns a rendered Django fragment — the same _section.html partial the first page already uses. No JSON, no client-side templates, no state to keep in sync.

The reason it can feel alarming is that it puts behaviour in markup, which looks superficially like the inline onclick attributes everyone is taught to avoid. The difference is that onclick embedded arbitrary JavaScript in HTML, whereas htmx attributes are a fixed declarative vocabulary — closer in spirit to what <form action method> has always done, generalised to any element and any verb. It is less magic than a framework, not more.

None of which means you should use it. The vanilla equivalent is roughly forty lines, and writing them teaches you the platform APIs — the better outcome if you're deliberately trying to get stronger at frontend. The honest recommendation: spend twenty minutes on the htmx docs so it becomes a known quantity rather than a scary one, then go vanilla as planned. If the loader later grows awkward — request cancellation, out-of-band updates, optimistic swaps — you'll recognise the moment htmx would have saved you, and adopting it then is a small change rather than a rewrite.

9.5 · Browser baseline

Edge, Chrome, Safari and Firefox, current and one previous version. Internet Explorer needs no consideration whatsoever — Microsoft ended support in June 2022 and it is absent from the modern web platform entirely. Everything recommended here (native nesting, content-visibility, :focus-visible, grid-template-rows transitions, AVIF, IntersectionObserver) is available across that baseline. The one item worth a manual check is -webkit-line-clamp, which is universally supported but still prefixed.

11

Security

A stated priority, and correctly so — a site that accepts image uploads and email addresses from the public has a real attack surface, and getting it right is both necessary and demonstrably good portfolio material.

11.1 · Django configuration

Run manage.py check --deploy before every deploy. It is built in, takes seconds, and checks most of the following automatically:

  • SECRET_KEY out of source and into an environment variable. It is currently hardcoded in settings.py and in git history — rotate it before launch, since anything committed is compromised.
  • DEBUG = False in production, and a populated ALLOWED_HOSTS. Debug pages leak settings, SQL and stack traces.
  • SECURE_HSTS_SECONDS, SECURE_SSL_REDIRECT, SESSION_COOKIE_SECURE, CSRF_COOKIE_SECURE, SECURE_CONTENT_TYPE_NOSNIFF.
  • A Content Security Policy. Easy here because the design self-hosts fonts and ships no third-party scripts — a strict policy costs nothing and blocks entire categories of injection.

11.2 · Uploads

The riskiest surface, and the one worth being thorough about.

  • Never trust the extension or the declared content type. Verify by decoding the file, which Django's ImageField does via Pillow.
  • Guard the decoder. Set Image.MAX_IMAGE_PIXELS and cap upload size. A 20KB PNG can declare dimensions that expand to gigabytes in memory — the classic decompression bomb.
  • If the in-browser tool outputs SVG, do not store it as SVG. SVG is an XML document that can carry <script>, and serving a user-supplied one from your own origin is stored cross-site scripting. Either rasterise it server-side or sanitise it hard. Accepting raster uploads only, as planned, sidesteps this entirely.
  • Serve uploads with X-Content-Type-Options: nosniff, and ideally from a separate domain or bucket so that even a mis-served file cannot run in the site's origin. Object storage handles this naturally.
  • Re-encode everything. Transcoding to AVIF/WebP for display incidentally strips anything hidden in the original's metadata.
  • Randomise stored filenames. Never use the contributor's filename on disk or in a URL.

11.3 · Abuse and rate limiting

  • Rate-limit reservations, submissions and reports — at Cloudflare, in Django (django-ratelimit), or both.
  • Honeypot field (§12.5 explains it) plus a minimum-time check on the submission form.
  • Email verification tokens should be signed and time-limited — use Django's signing module rather than storing guessable tokens.
  • Enumeration: keep responses identical whether or not an email is already known to the system, or the form becomes an oracle for which addresses have contributed.

11.4 · Data

IP addresses are personal data under UK GDPR. If they're stored for rate limiting, set a short retention period and truncate or hash them. The project's instinct to collect as little as possible is the right one and is much easier to hold from the start than to retrofit — every field not collected is a field that cannot leak.

11.5 · Dependencies

Turn on Dependabot in the repository settings — it is free, takes one click, and opens PRs for vulnerable packages. Add pip-audit to CI. Between them that is most of the supply-chain risk handled for about ten minutes of setup.

12

Decisions for Damian

Five questions that need frontend judgement rather than a recommendation from outside the project. Each is written to be readable on its own, with the tradeoffs laid out and a starting suggestion — but the call belongs to whoever is going to live with the layout.

12.1 · How wide is the artwork column?

One number has to serve a 320px phone and a 2560px monitor. Because anchors are stored as fractions of width, any width works mechanically — the road joins up regardless. What's actually at stake is legibility and consistency of presentation.

ApproachUpsideDownside
Full-bleed on mobile, clamped on desktop
clamp(18rem, 44vw, 32rem)		Uses all of a phone screen; leaves room for the numeral and metadata on desktopThe same artwork renders roughly twice as large on desktop as on a phone
Constant rendered width everywhere (e.g. 480px)Every visitor sees the work at the same size — the most gallery-like answerWastes most of a phone screen and looks lost on a large monitor
Proportional throughout
min(90vw, 32rem)		Simplest rule; scales smoothlyNothing anchors the reading size; wide screens get a very large column

The binding constraint is the desktop flanks: the metadata needs about 36ch and the numeral needs room beside it, so --road-w can't exceed roughly 45% of the viewport without squeezing them. The second consideration is that contributors will draw at whatever size they're working — so publish a canonical authoring width (1600px is a sensible number) so people know what they're designing for, and accept that phones show it smaller. Fine detail and any hand-drawn text will not survive a 320px render, and the guidelines should say so.

12.2 · What are the height limits?

Width is fixed and height varies "within limits" — this is about what sets those limits. Expressed as a ratio to the width, since that's the stable quantity.

  • Maximum is an experience question. Past roughly 1.5× the width, a single section takes more than a screen on desktop and several swipes on mobile, and the road stops reading as continuous — you're looking at one picture rather than travelling along something.
  • Minimum is the interesting one, and it isn't aesthetic. It's set by the metadata block. Title, author, a few lines of description and the action row need roughly 180–220px of vertical space beside the artwork on desktop. Any section shorter than that has metadata taller than its own artwork, which breaks the sticky positioning and pushes text into the neighbouring row. So the floor is a layout constraint, not a taste one — somewhere around 0.4× the width.

Starting suggestion: height between 0.4× and 1.5× the width. At a 1600px canonical authoring width that's 640px to 2400px. Worth building the layout first and then measuring, since the metadata block's real height is the thing that decides it.

12.3 · DaisyUI: keep or remove?

Currently installed, emitting about twenty colour tokens into the bundle, used by zero classes in the template. Held open pending your input.

The case against: its default component look is precisely the generic register §2 is trying to avoid, and it ships a parallel token system (--color-base-*, --color-primary) that will sit awkwardly beside the @theme tokens in §9.1. Two sources of truth for colour drift reliably.

The case for: accessible interactive primitives are genuinely fiddly to build well, and there are two here — the report dialog and the theme switcher. If you want it for those, the workable compromise is to use DaisyUI only there and never for the road, the metadata or the masthead. If it isn't being used for that, removing it is free and shrinks the bundle.

12.4 · Should the URL update as you scroll?

The appeal is that a reload returns you to the same stretch of road, and that sharing the address shares your position. The implementation caveat matters more than the decision: use history.replaceState, throttled to something like every 500ms or every few sections. pushState would stack a history entry per section and comprehensively destroy the back button — a genuinely common bug in infinite-scroll implementations.

The other consideration is that it competes with the per-section permalink. If every section already has a shareable UUID URL, a scroll-updating address may be redundant. Worth deciding which of the two is the primary sharing mechanism rather than shipping both and hoping.

12.5 · No-JS fallback: build it or not?

§8 argues that UUID permalinks don't actually force JS-only pagination, and that a cursor link costs little and preserves crawlability and screen-reader access. That said, it is extra surface to build and test, and skipping it is a defensible call for a two-person project.

If you skip it, the one thing worth keeping is server-rendered individual section pages, so sections stay findable and shareable even when the road itself needs JavaScript. That's most of the SEO and accessibility benefit for a fraction of the work.

Not a decision, just an explanation — honeypots

Mentioned in §11.3 and flagged as unfamiliar. A honeypot is a form field that real people never see and never fill in: you add an innocuous-looking input (say website), hide it in CSS, and set tabindex="-1" and aria-hidden="true" so keyboards and screen readers skip past it. Naive bots fill in every field they find, so any submission arriving with that field non-empty is discarded. Zero friction for real users, no accessibility cost when hidden properly, and it catches a surprising amount of low-effort spam. Pair it with a timestamp check — a form completed in under two seconds wasn't completed by a person. Neither stops a determined attacker, which is what the captcha and rate limiting are for; honeypots just remove the cheap noise for free. django-honeypot implements it if you'd rather not hand-roll.
